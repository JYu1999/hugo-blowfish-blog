title: "The First Step to Replacing Yourself: Learning to Express"

tags: ["Career"]

This year, by coincidence, I heard from two outstanding partners who told me they are constantly thinking about "how to replace themselves."

However, I later discovered that their execution methods were completely different, which sparked some thoughts.

One person's direction was "increasing ability levels." He believed that if he could learn more skills, he would be more competitive in the workplace and naturally avoid "being replaced."

The other person's direction was "liberating oneself from repetitive labor." He believed in finding the patterns in things and finding ways to automate them by any means so that he could have the time and energy to learn new skills.

Over time, I found that the first person didn't seem to be able to achieve "replacing himself" because he only focused on "learning more skills" without outsourcing the work, whether through AI or human assistance.

I've always felt that when everyone is working, the brain is running a "hidden algorithm that no one knows."

The judgments made daily, the ways problems are handled, the priorities valued, the risks intuitively avoided — these are not "results," but a set of rules hidden in the mind.

The problem is, most people can only state the goal: "I want this result."

But they can't describe the process: "How I got this result."

Perhaps sometimes when delegating to others, they might be able to "divine" your algorithm through reverse engineering or past experience.

But we all know that the most troublesome part of an algorithm is never the normal flow, but the edge cases.

Often, results look the same simply because of good luck, the right scenario, or few variables.

Once an exception, a gray area, or a moment of trade-off is encountered, those who haven't explained the logic clearly will cause the entire system to collapse.

If you cannot document the logic, edge cases, and style of this algorithm, over time your workload will only get heavier, and you will no longer have time to learn new skills.

And I think the best way is to actively write down your various decision logics and try to synthesize them into a set of "principles" in your daily life.

If you want AI or colleagues to share your workload, you must first become the translator of your own brain. Algorithms that cannot be documented cannot be scaled.

Writing yourself down might be the first step toward freedom.
