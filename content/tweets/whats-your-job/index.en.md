## What is your job?

> "When people introduce themselves, they don't actually state their job titles first. Instead, they talk about what they've been doing lately and what problems they are solving... People's identities are actually quite fluid."

When I first started looking for jobs, I used to care a lot about whether the job content matched the title.

But after working in startups for a few years, I deeply feel that job titles are just for reference.

Given the resources startups have, finding a dedicated person for every single task is obviously impossible.

Even putting resources aside, I found that the things I'm interested in change over time. Sometimes I care about performance, sometimes user experience, sometimes business value, and sometimes service stability. These simply cannot be covered by a single job role.

Ultimately, it all comes back to "solving problems": whether the company's current pain points overlap with my expertise, whether the company has the budget to do it, etc.

## How to Quickly Enter a New Field

Ashira has gone through multiple "cross-disciplinary" transitions in her career. Every time she changes departments, she has a set approach:

### Step 1: Build a Stakeholder Map

Identify who is the Decision Maker, the Subject Matter Expert, and the Blocker.

### Step 2: Extract Three Key Questions

- What are the challenges this organization is facing?
- What are its goals?
- What is currently lacking the most?

### Step 3: Don't be limited by job titles; proactively reposition yourself

> "Never be limited by Job Scope or Job Title... What you should actually be thinking is not just 'What should I do well in my current job?', but rather 'What is my boss, or even my boss's boss, thinking about, and how can I help them solve their problems?'"

I think it boils down to one sentence: "Clarify the goals, aim at the bottlenecks, and take initiative."

## The Mindset of Innovation

Ashira used Elon Musk as an example to explain the core mindset needed for innovation:

- First have a narrative, then find supporters, and then quickly trial and error.
- Failure doesn't equal failure; it's part of the progress.
- "Act first, talk later," to avoid the trap of perfectionism.

> "He has a lot of incredible ideas. He'll figure out a way to get a narrative out there first. He might not even have a product yet, but he'll try his best to rally people who support him... Even if the rocket explodes, he doesn't see it as a major failure, because it's actually progress as well."

This part inspired me a lot. I used to think I should prepare to a certain extent before doing something, as it would be more likely to succeed.

For instance, recently I wanted to introduce AI into our company. I thought I should write some small tools first, try them out, and make sure they work before pitching the idea to everyone.

But the Product Manager in our company had a different approach. He directly shared his user experience and implementation plan in a meeting, and even volunteered to host a Workshop.

To be honest, what he presented at first was quite incomplete. However, with every discussion, it felt more and more complete, and it gradually sparked everyone's interest.

I feel like this might be the way to survive in this highly volatile era? As long as there's a narrative and a vision, just dive in and try it out.
