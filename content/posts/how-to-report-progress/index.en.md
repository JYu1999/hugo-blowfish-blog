## When Should You Report Progress?

As a beginner in progress reporting, I might have a question:

> So under what circumstances do I need to report progress?

A common answer is: during regular meetings.

Every department will more or less have Standup Meetings or weekly meetings. You can share the difficulties you encountered, what problems were solved, and what risks were seen during the regular meeting.

I think this method is very suitable for beginners because there is no need to make a special appointment with the supervisor, and other colleagues in the company can also know.

But you also have to be careful. The function of regular meetings is usually to synchronize progress. If too much time is spent sharing the details of personal tasks, others may be somewhat impatient.

And if we report some situations, the supervisor usually needs to make a decision. If everyone speaks at the meeting, the supervisor has to make many decisions at the meeting without time to collect more information or think, which may also cause discomfort to the supervisor.

So a more recommended direction of thinking is: **Start from the manager's personality and anticipate when they want to hear a report.**

### The Reporting Matrix of Psychological Safety and Trust

Everyone has a different level of psychological concern about risks. Some people are more optimistic, while others are more worrisome.

And this level of concern about risk is everyone's psychological safety.

This kind of psychological safety is often something we cannot change. It is a set of values developed based on all his past experiences.

So when facing the supervisor's psychological safety, we can only observe and adopt different strategies.

Another factor is trust, which is whether the supervisor can safely hand over tasks to employees.

When first joining a company, trust is usually low, but it can be accumulated slowly over time.

And the key to accumulation happens to be "progress reporting".

So how do we know when to report progress through psychological safety and trust?

You can refer to the following figure:

![Reporting Matrix](/posts/how-to-report-progress/image.png)

#### Low Trust: High Frequency Reporting, Ask for Decisions

When the supervisor doesn't trust you yet, you must report daily and ask for instructions on everything, big or small, and let the supervisor make decisions to accumulate trust.

Psychological safety is not a key determinant here, after all, psychological safety takes time to observe.

#### High Trust + Low Psychological Safety: Daily Sync, Align Together

When your results are slowly showing, and the supervisor affirms your ability and reporting ability, you can slowly change to this mode.

However, although the supervisor trusts you, he himself is easily anxious.

So you still need **"simple daily synchronization"** to reassure him. You can handle matters within your authority and inform him afterwards, but you need to align immediately when encountering changes.

#### High Trust + High Psychological Safety: Weekly Sync, Autonomous Decisions

This is the ideal state. Just report "weekly", decide for yourself within your authority, and make suggestions for matters outside your authority for the supervisor's reference.

## Structure of Progress Reporting

Ok, we already know when to report work, but how exactly should we report it? Is there a structure that is easier for beginners to get started with?

First, we need to determine whether the current situation is as expected, or if it is actually not as expected (e.g., delays, technical difficulties).

### On Track / As Expected

![Progress reporting on track](/posts/how-to-report-progress/image2.png)

If things are going well, then simply synchronize the current results, explain the tasks for the next stage, and propose potential risks and preparation measures currently seen.

If things are going well because of your efforts, you can add whether you have resolved any issues in the process.

### Off Track / Not As Expected

No one celebrates Chinese New Year every day; projects will always have times when they explode.

Of course, no one wants the project to explode, but if it really happens, you can change your mindset.

"Not as expected" is often a good opportunity to perform. Standing up when things are not as expected, eliminating the problem, and showing a responsible attitude will make others impressed with you.

The focus of progress reporting when "not as expected" is to show **how to close the gap**.

![Progress reporting not as expected](/posts/how-to-report-progress/image3.png)

When reporting, you can refer to the following structure:

1.  **Gap Analysis:** How much is the specific difference? How large is the impact scope?
2.  **Cause Explanation:** Why did it happen?
3.  **Actions Taken:** What has been done to stop the bleeding or remedy?
4.  **Results and Expectations:** What are the results after the remedy? When is it expected to catch up with the progress?
5.  **Next Steps and Help Needed:** What other risks are there? What resources are needed?

Through the above structure, you can clearly let the supervisor know that you not only fully understand the gap, but have also formulated a plan and taken action. Instead of waiting for him to find out before dealing with it. "Trust" in you will instantly rise a lot.

I want to remind you again here that the focus of reporting when not as expected lies in how to **eliminate the gap**.

We must have three basic attitudes towards gaps:

![Attitude towards gaps](/posts/how-to-report-progress/image4.png)

First, **Outcome First**. No need to spend a lot of time finding someone to blame, explaining details, or explaining why the gap occurred.

Next, show the action and determination to try to catch up with the gap, rather than just explaining the gap.

Finally, think from a global perspective. Even if the gap is caught up this week, is it possible that the project will still be delayed?
