Recently, I was discussing a topic with a colleague. We had different understandings of the issue, so he went back and discussed it with several friends.

A few days later, he approached me to discuss it again. His opening line was:

> I met with xxx, he is a [role] at [Big Company]; I also asked aa, who works at bbb in the US... (and so on). I can tell you the conclusion: basically, your idea is bullshit.

I was quite dumbfounded when I heard that. I was just hoping to get an answer, not trying to win an argument...

Setting feelings aside, I think this is a suboptimal way to discuss things.

There are two main reasons:

## 1. Just give the conclusion?

"Just give me the conclusion" sounds cool, like you're cutting to the chase.

But if that sentence is followed by a statement negating the other person, one should be careful about using it.

Because once you state that conclusion, you are essentially declaring: "In the following discussion, I am the opposition, and I will be responsible for proving you wrong."

Placing yourself on the opposite side right at the start of a discussion doesn't really help. For more sensitive people, their defense mechanisms might kick in immediately.

## 2. Fallacies

Let's look at what a fallacy is:

> A fallacy is the use of invalid or otherwise faulty reasoning, or "wrong moves", in the construction of an argument, which may appear stronger than it really is if the fallacy is not spotted. (Source: [List of fallacies - Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_fallacies))

In this case, my colleague's response corresponds to two common fallacies: **Appeal to Authority** and **Appeal to the People** (Bandwagon).

### Appeal to Authority

Appeal to authority is a form of argument in which the opinion of an authority on a topic is used as evidence to support an argument. It assumes that just because someone is an authority (or has a specific title or status), their view must be correct, ignoring the need to examine the evidence and logic behind it.

The problem here is that "who said it" does not determine "whether it is true". There are several reasons:

- Even experts in the field can make mistakes.
- Did the expert truly understand the context? Is it possible the description was too biased or lacked premises, leading the expert to an incorrect judgment?
- Sometimes we only see the expert's "decision", not necessarily understanding their reasons.

This reminds me of discussing technical architecture with someone in the past, who told me:

> Because [Big Shot] uses it this way, I think we should follow their approach.

But actually, the big shot might never have encountered our scenario, or they might have omitted some details for the sake of easier communication.

These are things that cannot be judged solely by the result.

### Appeal to the People

Appeal to the people, also known as the "bandwagon fallacy", is the belief that "because everyone says so or does so, it must be right".

My colleague mentioned he asked "several friends" and listed a string of names, implying: "See, so many people disagree with you, you must be wrong."

There are two problems with this line of reasoning:

1.  **Echo Chamber**: People who gather together usually have similar ideas. Could it be that asking people around you naturally yields opinions similar to your own?
2.  **Truth is not a democratic vote**: History is full of examples where what the majority believed at the time was later proven wrong (e.g., Geocentric model).

In technical or professional discussions, the quantity of opinions cannot make up for the lack of quality. If the people asked are all basing their views on the same incorrect premises and assumptions, then five hundred people saying the same thing still won't make it a fact.

## What happened next?

It feels like I'm roasting my colleague, but he did actually follow up with his explanation.

I partially agreed with his reasoning, but I also felt my core point hadn't been addressed. So I stated my perspective again and explained the parts I found unreasonable.

At this point, his feedback was:

> Isn't what I just said very clear? If you still can't understand, well, maybe I'm just too bad at explaining things.

I was genuinely asking sincerely, but I sensed he felt I was playing dumb, so I just accepted his explanation.

However, this incident gave me some additional inspiration.

## I don't care what they think?

My colleague's final way of speaking reminded me of a meme:

<img src="/tweets/appeal-to-authority/resize.jpeg" alt="Keanu Reeves - You are right" style="max-width: 400px; width: 100%;" />

Actually, I used to have this mindset for a while.

But later I discovered that interests between people are conflicting yet overlapping.

With people completely unrelated to you, sure, you can ignore what they say.

But if you share a common goal, like making the company better or making your family better, this way of thinking might not apply.

Because the reality is: regardless of what the other person thinks, if you want to push things forward, you inevitably have to cooperate with them.

If you can't face different opinions head-on and work together to come up with a conclusion, but instead adopt an attitude of "well, I've said what I needed to say," I think cooperation becomes very difficult.

Because when you adopt this attitude, others will definitely feel it. Over time, they won't want to offer their perspectives anymore, feeling that doing so will only be seen as looking for trouble.

Thus, opacity begins to appear. Opacity leads to distrust, and distrust leads to cooperation becoming difficult... it's only a matter of time.

But I think it is worth noting here: facing it head-on is one thing, but remember not to take it personally.

## Follow-up Actions

I've been wondering, do I need to find that colleague to discuss this again?

After writing this, I feel it is necessary. The reason is that one of my requirements for my own professional standards is to express my views as openly and transparently as possible.

Of course, not blurting out everything without filter, but converting it into a more tactful delivery.

Why do I value "transparency" so much? Mainly because of Google's article [《Understand team effectiveness》](https://rework.withgoogle.com/intl/en/guides/understanding-team-effectiveness).

This article describes a project called "Project Aristotle", which was Google's research into what differentiates high-performing teams from average ones.

The research team found five key elements of high-performing teams:

1.  **Psychological Safety**

    Team members feel safe to take risks (like asking questions, admitting mistakes, offering new ideas) without being embarrassed, punished, or seen as incompetent.

    > In a team with high psychological safety, teammates feel safe to take risks around their team members. They feel confident that no one on the team will embarrass or punish anyone else for admitting a mistake, asking a question, or offering a new idea.

2.  **Dependability**

    Team members reliably complete quality work on time (vs the opposite - shirking responsibilities).

    > On dependable teams, members reliably complete quality work on time (vs the opposite - shirking responsibilities).

3.  **Structure and Clarity**

    An individual’s understanding of job expectations, the process for fulfilling these expectations, and the consequences of one’s performance are important for team effectiveness.

    > An individual’s understanding of job expectations, the process for fulfilling these expectations, and the consequences of one’s performance are important for team effectiveness.

4.  **Meaning**

    Finding a sense of purpose in either the work itself or the output is important for team effectiveness.

    > Finding a sense of purpose in either the work itself or the output is important for team effectiveness.

5.  **Impact**

    The results of one’s work, the subjective judgment that your work is making a difference, is important for teams.

    > The results of one’s work, the subjective judgment that your work is making a difference, is important for teams.

Google researchers believe the most important one is "Psychological Safety".

This coincides with my understanding. Only when team members believe they won't be humiliated, over-interpreted, or viewed as incompetent for speaking up, will they feel safe to speak. And when team members speak freely, the team's transparency increases. The higher the team transparency, the clearer the decision-making, and the better the company becomes.

In the TED talk by Amy Edmondson, who coined the concept of "Psychological Safety" ([Building a psychologically safe workplace | Amy Edmondson | TEDxHGSE](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LhoLuui9gX8)), she explicitly suggests three things individuals or leaders can do:

1.  Frame work as a learning problem: Not just an execution problem, emphasizing that we are here to solve unknown challenges.
2.  Acknowledge your own fallibility: Show that you are not omniscient.
3.  Model curiosity: Ask a lot of questions, encourage dialogue.

I feel that as a junior employee, I might not be able to improve the entire company atmosphere or anything.

But what I hope is, at least starting from myself, to do what is right and truly good for the team. This is what I consider my professional standard as a part of the team.

Perhaps after I say this, I really will be humiliated, but at least I can be sure that this environment might not be the place where I should stay long-term.

## Conclusion

Writing this article isn't to condemn that colleague. Quite the opposite, I learned a lot of concepts from the process of discussing with him. To some extent, it also debunked my own "fallacies", so I am actually grateful.

After all, as humans, it's normal to have fallacies in our thinking sometimes. Through exchange, discussion, and brainstorming with each other, making everyone better together is what I think is more important.

In addition, writing this article also serves as a warning to myself to pay more attention when discussing topics with others in the future.
