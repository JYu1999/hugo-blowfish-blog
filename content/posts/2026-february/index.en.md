### 🤖 AI Research

**Agent Skill Experiment**

This month, I mainly researched Agent Skill—organizing the usage of open-source projects into Skills, so that when AI needs to use the tool, it can directly refer to the Skill to operate, without having to explore the documentation from scratch.

I tried to organize YTDLP into a Skill, and the effect was quite good. But considering that each download consumes Tokens, and existing free tools can actually meet basic download needs, this Skill might be more suitable for "batch download" scenarios. For one or two videos usually, I'll just use online tools directly.

**OpenSpec Experiment**

I mentioned wanting to research OpenSpec last month, and I actually tried it this month.

Compared to the BMAD method, BMAD's confirmation is very detailed (suitable for brainstorming new features or new products) but consumes a lot of Tokens; OpenSpec saves Tokens and is more suitable for situations where you already have a rough idea and just need to further refine the plan.

However, the result of this experiment was not ideal—I gave too many requirements at once and did not ask the AI to follow the conventions of existing projects. As a result, the proposed solution was too risky, so I simply deleted it. I will continue to try later, but won't treat it as a main research direction.

### 🔧 Tools to Improve Team Efficiency

The company's release process is currently entirely manual: opening MRs, organizing commit messages, writing MR descriptions, sending Slack notifications... The steps are cumbersome and take a lot of time each time.

This month, by combining MCP and Skill, I built an automated tool. The rough process is as follows:

1. Automatically compare the differences between stage and master to confirm if there is new progress to release.
2. If so, automatically open an MR in GitLab that complies with the specifications (including title and description).
3. Merge after confirmation by engineers and PM.
4. After deployment is complete, automatically send the organized message to the Slack release channel.

This tool helped me save the most time-consuming parts: opening MRs and writing Slack notifications. The tool is currently completed but has not yet been practically tested in an official release. I will check its effectiveness during the next release.

## Yes Man!

Since joining my current company, I've been greatly influenced by my manager—he places a high value on risk and requires us to see as many aspects and avoid risks as much as possible before doing anything. This habit extended into my life, making me think the risks were very high before I even tried many things, and then I just let them go.

This month, I want to make some changes: if something is within my capabilities, potentially helpful, and I haven't even tried it yet, I will give it a chance.

For example, I've always kind of wanted to join a language study group, but in the past, I always felt "a bunch of strangers is very uncomfortable," although I've never actually gone. Also, for minor plumbing and electrical repairs at home, I was always afraid of breaking things, but in reality, even if I did break it, I could just hire someone to fix it. If I learned how to do it, it might save a lot of trouble instead.

This thought was triggered once during my trip to Japan. I went to a Muay Thai gym for a trial class, and an uncle in the gym who immigrated from China to Japan asked me if I had ever thought about working in Japan. My first reaction was: "No, my Japanese is too poor." He said that actually, if you have sufficient professional skills, you can come. At that moment, I suddenly realized—I was making excuses again. The real question I should ask myself is: do I really want to do this? If the answer is yes, then difficulties are meant to be overcome, not to stop myself from taking action.

This reminded me of an article by Vincent. He mentioned that good students are good at planning and collecting data, but in the execution phase, they often procrastinate—because as long as they don't do it, they won't be graded by reality. The reversal in thinking is: change the "100-point deduction system" to a "0-point addition system." Every attempt adds points, even failure, because it means you actually did it. Another concept is to seek rapid feedback rather than trying to get it right at once—just like agile development, breaking tasks down, doing them first, and then adjusting, is the way to go far.

Starting in March, I want to move in this direction: try first, then adjust.

## The 10,000-Hour Rule

This month in Muay Thai class, after sparring with a student, he said our skills were "completely not in the same dimension." This surprised me a bit. I don't have any special talent, and my practice method is very simple—just signing up for classes, attending on time, three to five times a week, and continuing for over two years. There were brief interruptions in between, but I still continued to practice basics and physical fitness. Just like that, there's no special trick.

Recently, I saw a YouTuber sharing his way of learning Japanese: there is no special method, just massive input—reading books in Japanese, watching videos in Japanese, watching dramas in Japanese, and looking up what he doesn't understand. In the end, he scored full marks on the JLPT N1 and is now studying abroad in Japan. He said the key to not learning Japanese well is not the study method, but an "overwhelming lack of time spent."

These two things combined gave me great inspiration: the result of learning is like multiplication—time investment × efficiency = outcome. When the time investment is insufficient, no matter how much you improve efficiency, the effect is limited.

I observe a phenomenon in this era where "efficiency anxiety" is oversold: AI is going to replace you, you'll fall behind if you don't keep up, using the wrong learning method is a waste of time... making it seem like you must improve efficiency every moment. But I think a better strategy is instead: maintain your own pace, invest enough time, and wait until you truly hit a bottleneck before researching how to improve efficiency. Excessive pursuit of efficiency easily leads to burnout, and once you burn out, you can't continue to invest time, which makes you fall behind in the long run.

Therefore, subsequently in learning, I will deliberately reduce the "types of learning" and concentrate my time on a few things I truly want to do well.

## 🗣️ Practicing Languages

While traveling in Japan, there were several times when the other person spoke English to me, but my brain suddenly crashed—I couldn't even say basic greetings fluently. That moment made me realize that I hadn't practiced speaking for way too long.

The plan is simple: fix 15 to 30 minutes every day to use an AI speaking APP to practice either English or Japanese, forcing myself to speak every day. From the most basic greetings and self-introductions to specific scenarios like airports and cafes, following the APP's training plan.

In addition, I also want to try "Shadowing"—picking some favorite Japanese or English Podcasts or videos, mimicking the speaker's accent and rhythm, so that my way of speaking becomes more natural.

The goal is clear: make "speaking up" a natural thing, rather than "knowing how to say it but just not being able to say it."
