## Do we really need to roll up our sleeves?

After consuming this content, I felt conflicted.

Standing before me is a high wall named AI, a field where even experts feel they are falling behind.

And it’s not just AI itself; using AI requires fundamental knowledge as support, so I still have to catch up on other basics.

Can I choose not to learn? But everyone says it’s super important, and if I don't learn, I’ll be replaced.

Can I actually learn it all? Do I have that much time and energy? Do I still have time to learn other things I'm interested in?

Is the best way to face this giant wave of AI really to "Roll up your sleeves to not fall behind"?

Even if I catch up in the short term, how long do I have to keep chasing? Will I burn out? Will I lose out on other aspects of life?

## AI Fatigue

A Forbes article touched on this situation: [《Why 'AI Fatigue' Is Wearing You Down And How To Beat It》](https://www.forbes.com/sites/carolinecastrillon/2025/06/24/why-ai-fatigue-is-wearing-you-down-and-how-to-beat-it/).

**AI Fatigue** is defined as **employees and organizations feeling exhausted due to the explosive growth of AI tools, excessive pressure for change, lack of support, and fear of unemployment.**

The article clearly mentions five key causes of AI Fatigue:

1. **Rapid pace of change**: Frequent updates leave employees no time to master existing tools.

2. **Lack of appropriate support and training**: Companies often just give orders without providing enough resources to help employees transition.

3. **Lack of job security**: Employees learn AI sometimes not for efficiency, but out of a survival instinct.

4. **Opaque internal communication**: Leadership fails to explain the specific reasons for introducing AI, leading employees to view it as an extra burden.

5. **Unrealistic expectations**: Media and upper management mythologize AI, leading to disappointment when tools don’t yield immediate results.

I resonate deeply with this. In fact, I learned AI simply because of "3. Lack of job security," not out of curiosity, efficiency, or encountering real problems.

And the hype on social media gave me "5. Unrealistic expectations," thinking AI is already so powerful that it doesn't need humans, and I might be replaced next month.

But the fact is: it’s not that powerful yet.

## What is my strategy?

### Project Based Learning (PBL)

I strongly agree with a conclusion in the Forbes article:

> Instead of trying to understand AI technology comprehensively, concentrate on practical applications that solve real problems in your work.

We don't actually need to spend time learning things we won't use. Focus on solving the immediate problem first, and if you have time, try to see if AI can make the process of solving that problem better.

For example, I use Cursor, Antigravity, Gemini, and ChatGPT, and I have tried developing some small AI applications.

But I won’t go out of my way to study terms like Sub-agent, A2A, or MCP. The reason is simple: I don't have a need for them.

Take MCP for example. In my work context, manual copy-pasting versus a model calling a tool to find information makes almost no difference in efficiency. The model might even find the wrong thing. So why should I spend a lot of time researching how to configure it, authorize it, etc.?

Faced with these new terms, I usually just get a general overview so I can pull it out if I need it later.

Some might say: "You don't know how good it is because you haven't used it. If you try it, you might love it." True, but I have the following reasons:
1. **Let the bullets fly for a while**: Sometimes new technologies are just hype, not to mention flash-in-the-pan tech (like Prompt Engineering). Many things we don't need to solve ourselves; maybe someone will build a service for us to use (possibly even for free).
2. **Productivity improvements don't have to be instant**: If something is truly good, I will eventually know about it. I can use it when it becomes a household name; I don't need to be the first to try everything.

Actually, it's not just AI. I tend to lean towards PBL for learning any knowledge now. Set a practical problem first, then extend research directly from that issue.

### Same as Ever

The book *Same as Ever* mentions:

> Change is exciting and surprising, so it captures our attention. But behaviors that never change are history's most powerful lessons because they preview what is to come, mirroring your future and everyone else's future. No matter who you are, where you are from, how old you are, or how much money you make, human behavior contains timeless lessons, and this is the most important thing you can learn.
>
> This concept is simple but easily overlooked. Once you grasp it, you can better understand your life, understand why the world looks the way it does, and face the future more comfortably.
>
> Things that never change are important because you can know with confidence how they will shape the future. Bezos said you can't imagine future Amazon customers not wanting low prices or fast delivery, so he could invest heavily in those things.

I find this very suitable for dealing with this increasingly fast-changing era.

Instead of focusing on "what is changing," it is better to focus on mastering the things that do not change.

## Conclusion

In this era of rapid technological change, feeling anxious is human nature. But we don't need to drift with the tide, nor do we need to force ourselves to become experts in every new tool. Through Project Based Learning, we can return learning to needs itself, making every investment meaningful. At the same time, don't forget to focus on those "Same as Ever" things; they are the foundation for us to settle down in this changing world.

I hope this article gives you a little strength if you are also feeling anxious. Let us go further, at our own pace.
