I joined my current company in November 2023, and as of January 2026, two years and three months have passed in the blink of an eye.

This period has been a significant "run-in" period in my career. Looking back, I've navigated quite a few challenges and made mistakes that now seem rather "basic." To remind my future self and to organize my current state of mind, I want to document in detail a few things I feel I didn't do well during this time.

## 1. Slow Understanding of the Product's Big Picture

When I first joined, I had a somewhat passive mindset, operating in a state of "working on whatever ticket I was assigned."

Although the technical threshold of our product isn't extremely high, the business logic is incredibly complex. It involves not just the product code itself, but also various server configurations, collaboration between multiple microservices, and the intricate connections between these components. It wasn't until now, more than two years later, that I dared to say I have a fairly complete understanding of the entire architecture and functionality.

### Why was this a mistake?

**Impact on collaborating departments**: During cross-departmental discussions, the product team or other departments often have questions about technical implementation. If an engineer responds with "I don't know" every time and has to go back and check the documentation before replying, it significantly lowers the trust of other departments.

**Low Development and Debugging Efficiency**: If you are unfamiliar with the architecture, you cannot quickly pinpoint the problem when developing new features or troubleshooting. Sometimes, just finding the location of an error takes hours, which is a huge drain on team efficiency.

**Low Irreplaceability of Personal Value**: If I only focus on the knowledge from the issues I've handled, my professional barrier to entry will be very low. If I don't study the product thoroughly, a new colleague who is more willing to study the architecture can quickly surpass what I've accumulated over the past two years.

## 2. Forgetting the Core of "Fast Value Delivery"

I used to have a bad habit of thinking about requirements too "ambitiously." When executing, I often complicated things by pursuing the perfect solution, leading to delivery times far exceeding expectations.

### The Lesson of Server Management

The most obvious case was a server management issue I was responsible for. That issue was critical, but I initially failed to perceive its urgency and set a long-term goal of one year for myself. However, the timeline expected by my supervisor was half a year, because that was the company's urgent priority.

At the time, I was too idealistic, delving into how to use Ansible for refined management and writing complex automated Bash Scripts. But my supervisor's core requirement was actually very simple: "**Someone to manage the servers and be able to quickly set up a new environment.**"

As a result, I spent a lot of time researching tools but delayed solving the core problem. For the company, this was zero value—even if I learned new technologies, failing to achieve the goal meant a waste of resources.

### Realization: Time is Risk

That incident made me deeply realize: **Time is risk**. The modern business environment changes extremely fast. If you need a year to create value, you might face variables like project cancellation, the supervisor losing patience, or the emergence of competitors in the meantime. If no tangible results are delivered in the end, all the effort and hard work during the process will not be seen.

I now believe: **Focus on the company's most critical core goals first, create value first, and then evaluate subsequent optimizations when there is leeway.**

## 3. Only Raising Problems, Not Actively Solving Them

This was a major mistake in my work experience over the past two years. I could often keenly detect problems in the system and raise them, but I often stopped at just raising them, leaving the problems unresolved.

### Solving Problems is the Proof of Value

I later reflected and discovered that identifying the problem is only the first step; actively solving it is the key to proving personal value. This not only improves the operational efficiency of the office but also becomes a powerful achievement for resumes and job hunting. Now when I see a problem, I first ask myself: "Is there anything I can do about this?" Take the initiative instead of waiting for instructions.

## 4. Lack of Upward Management and Communication

Upward management is the part I think I performed worst in the past, mainly reflected in progress reporting and trust building.

### 1. Progress Reports Should Not Just Be a Checklist

I used to prefer concise reporting in meetings, but after observing high-performing colleagues, I realized: **No one can know how many difficult problems you have silently solved behind the scenes if you don't tell them.** If you don't speak up, no one will know how many security and scalability details you considered behind a seemingly simple CRUD feature. Actively communicating these thoughts allows your contributions to be seen.

### 2. Throw Out Uncertainties Promptly

I used to be in the habit of thinking a problem through and listing all options before discussing it. But actually, throwing out the problem when you are uncertain can often quickly garner suggestions from others, or even reveal that there is already a ready-made solution on the third-party service side, preventing yourself from drilling into a dead end in the wrong direction.

### 3. Building Trust and Participation in Decision Making

The point of upward management is to make the supervisor feel "**everything is under control.**" In the early stages of a project, even if you already have a preferred plan in mind, you should frequently give the supervisor decision-making power (for example, providing two or three decision points every day). When the supervisor gets used to your reporting frequency and participates in your decision-making process, they will have a stronger sense of trust in you, which is crucial for future promotions and striving for authority.

## 5. Having Unrealistic Romantic Expectations of the Company

I used to have a romantic imagination: as long as I silently do good for the company and help it become better, the company will naturally thank me and reward me. But the reality is, the company will think that is the duty you should fulfill for your salary.

### Venn Diagram and Interest Balance

Now I use a "Venn diagram" to think: one circle is "what the company wants," and the other is "what I want."

**Maximize the Intersection**: 60% to 80% of what I do in the company should be things I am interested in or want to learn.

**Stop Expecting Meaningless Thanks**: Don't do things the company wants but I don't want just to "exchange for thanks." That will only lead to disappointed expectations.

### Continuously Think About "How to Eliminate Myself"

In my work, I started thinking about how to "eliminate" my current self through automation or process optimization. When I can quickly complete current tasks, I can free up time to learn new technologies or rest. This creates a positive flywheel: **Save time -> Learn new knowledge -> Create greater value -> Gain more competitiveness**.

## 6. Reflection on "Sweeping Only the Snow in Front of One's Own Door"

Limited by the belief that "my ability is limited, I should focus on my own affairs," I rarely took the initiative to help colleagues in the past. I originally thought that everyone doing their own job well was the best state for the team.

But after observing new colleagues, I found that actively helping others can significantly increase exposure and importance within the team. When you are publicly thanked in a meeting, or others silently note your assistance, these translate into workplace credit and networking benefits. I am trying to adjust my mindset and offer help more actively where I can.

## 7. Continuously Update Resume and Work Log

This is an important realization recently. When it comes time to discuss salary or organize a resume, human memory is unreliable.

### The Value of Work Logs Lies In:

**Recording Decision Details**: Helps you report with evidence during meetings.

**Quantifying Contributions**: Allows supervisors and future interviewers to clearly see key problems you have solved.

**AI Analysis**: In the AI era, detailed logs allow AI to quickly summarize achievements and analyze pain points for you.

## Conclusion

I have started correcting some of these reflections, while others I am still working hard to overcome.

Two years and three months is neither long nor short, just enough for a newcomer to see the rules of the workplace clearly. For me, this is not just technical progress, but also a reshaping of my mindset. I hope these lessons can become nutrients for my growth in the next stage. I also hope this article can provide some reference for you who are also confused in your career.
